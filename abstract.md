**Title**: Rabbits, wildcats, pond turtles and a free-flowing river: An open connectivity screen for trophic and hydrological rewilding in the Greater Côa Valley, Portugal

**Format**: Poster. **Series**: Lands in Motion.

**Author**: Linda Angulo Lopez, Rewilding Portugal. ORCID 0009-0009-5689-7148.

## Abstract
Rewilding Portugal conducted a connectivity study in the Greater Côa Valley in northern Portugal. The Côa River is free-flowing because a 1995 civic campaign, "As gravuras não sabem nadar" (rock art cannot swim), stopped a dam to save its rock art. [**Rock art is linked to the project due to this history? and the rock-art corridor is the most important connectivity corridor for the 3 spp?**]. Using circuit theory, we analyzed connectivity across three species: the European rabbit (*Oryctolagus cuniculus*), an Iberian Peninsula keystone species, near-threatened in its native range; the European wildcat (*Felis silvestris*), which depends on the rabbit as a prey species; and the pond turtle (*Emys orbicularis*), which needs pools that retain water through summer. A quarter of the study area lies in Spain, where Natura 2000 sites and natural parks connect to Portuguese sites across the border, but the current model stops at the border. We ran one screen for all three species on an open, watershed-scale connectivity workflow: 10,230 km², ten focal species, circuit theory in pure Python, with penalties for recent fire, 22 field-recorded barriers, and two UNESCO World Heritage areas. We paired the maps [**which maps?**] with new drawings of the three animals in the valley's Iron Age fine-line style [**state why!**]. The study found that rabbit release candidates were limited to scrub-pasture mosaic inside the top third of the wildcat's land corridors: 1,045 km² in ten priority survey blocks, 87% of it on hunting land. The pond turtle screen found 96 pools with water year-round, 48 on better-connected rivers, 13 inside the Côa rock art core, and only one within 2 km of a rabbit area. Wildcat conservation relies on neutering domestic cats and monitoring mesopredators. For the next phase of the study, we propose (i) a rabbit baseline survey run with hunters, (ii) an August pool survey prior to turtle release, (iii) adding both species to the geospatial and data analysis workflow, and (iv) a transfrontier study that extends the connectivity model into Spain.
